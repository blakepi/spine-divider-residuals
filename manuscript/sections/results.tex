\section{Results}

\subsection{Reference cases reproduce the intended passive ordering}

The baseline reference configuration generated the intended progression from near-isopotential head-dendrite responses to strong local isolation (Figures~\ref{fig:architecture} and \ref{fig:baseline_validation}). The simulations used the baseline parameters in Table~\ref{tab:parameters}, $\Delta t=0.01$ ms, a single head synapse at 20 ms, and the analysis-defined 50 ms metric window. All nine low-, intermediate-, and high-isolation target values passed the workflow-specified tolerance rule.

The low-$\rhoload$ case produced $\rhoload=0.00881$, $\Ghd=0.984$, $\Ghs=0.357$, and $\Ah=1.82$ mV. The intermediate case produced $\rhoload=0.115$, $\Ghd=0.751$, $\Ghs=0.273$, and $\Ah=2.36$ mV. The high-$\rhoload$ case produced $\rhoload=1.32$, $\Ghd=0.102$, $\Ghs=0.0376$, and $\Ah=15.1$ mV. These target cases support implementation consistency for the reference equations, units, synaptic normalization, solver, and metric definitions in the compact passive architecture; they do not calibrate the model to a specific biological neuron.

The same traces show why the divider residual is needed. The baseline divider predictions were 0.991, 0.897, and 0.431 for the low, intermediate, and high cases. The corresponding residuals $\Ghd-\Gamma_{\mathrm{div}}$ were -0.00729, -0.1458, and -0.3286. The low case was close to the DC divider, whereas the intermediate and high transient peak ratios fell well below it.

\subsection{The analytic divider is a reference, not the peak-response result}

Across 3,718 deterministic design-derived residual rows, the median absolute residual from the divider was 0.0548, the mean absolute residual was 0.0734, and the root-mean-square residual was 0.103. The maximum absolute residual was 0.492. Residuals were negative in 3,680/3,718 rows and positive in 38 rows, so the DC divider overpredicted this separate-peak conductance-based transfer metric in most designed rows (Figure~\ref{fig:divider_residuals}). Pooled values describe this deterministic design table rather than a biological population.

The largest departures were consistent with the intended distinction between a DC reference and a transient conductance-driven peak response. The matched-neck heterogeneous-load sweep produced the largest absolute separate-peak residual, with $\rhoload=0.452$, observed $\Ghd=0.196$, divider prediction 0.689, and residual -0.492. Fixed-load high-$\rhoload$ rows also showed large relative departures.

Matched-time and integrated controls showed that residual magnitude is metric-dependent. In the three baseline reference cases, area-under-curve residuals were closer to the divider for intermediate/high cases than the primary separate-peak residual, whereas the matched-head-peak control emphasized timing and downstream filtering. The largest matched-neck heterogeneous-load case showed the same metric dependence: the separate-peak residual was -0.492, the matched-head-peak residual was -0.637, and the AUC residual was -0.101. Thus the divider overprediction statement applies to this separate-peak conductance-response metric, not to every possible transfer summary.

Excluding exploratory rows left the same conclusion: 3584 non-exploratory rows had median absolute residual 0.0563, mean absolute residual 0.0748, and only two positive residual rows. The divider relation therefore remains the correct analytic baseline, but the residual field is a design-specific simulation-domain map.

\subsection{Load normalization is useful but compact}

The fixed-load geometry sweep behaved as a constructional sanity check. Longer and narrower necks increased $\Rneck$, increased head depolarization, and decreased downstream transfer. Because $\Rin$ was fixed, $\rhoload$ and $\Rneck$ had identical rank ordering. This result should not be read as evidence that the ratio adds information under fixed-load conditions.

The matched-neck experiment held $\Rneck=100$ M$\Omega$ while varying dendritic leak from 0.1 to 30 nS (Figure~\ref{fig:fixedload}). Under this manipulation, neck resistance alone could not rank the cases because there was only one neck-resistance value. Changing the dendritic load changed $\Rin$, and therefore changed $\rhoload$ and downstream transfer. The load term is therefore mechanistically meaningful, but only within the target it was designed to represent.

Descriptor comparisons made this compactness explicit. For non-exploratory local transfer, the load-normalized ratio and $\Gamma_{\mathrm{div}}$ had the same absolute Spearman association because the divider is a monotone transform of the ratio, but the analytic divider was the better direct scalar prediction (cross-validated RMSE 0.052 versus 0.091 for raw $\rhoload$). The log component pair improved on raw $\rhoload$ but did not outperform the analytic divider for first-order local transfer. Thus the ratio is useful as the coordinate defining the divider, not as a universally superior scalar.

\paragraph{External summary anchors as a bounded load-normalization decision gate.}
A Codex-only external-data gate recovered summary-level external data, not paired per-spine Harnett measured rows. Three summary-usable rows were eligible: a Harnett model/summary high-isolation anchor, a Popovic measured summary anchor, and a Popovic model-fit reference \citep{Harnett2012SpineAmplification,Popovic2015}. Source-native calculations placed the Harnett anchor at $\rhoload=4$ and $\Gamma_{\mathrm{div}}=0.200$ (high), the Popovic measured summary at $\rhoload=0.0982$ and $\Gamma_{\mathrm{div}}=0.911$ (low), and the Popovic model-fit row at $\rhoload=0.0718$ and $\Gamma_{\mathrm{div}}=0.933$ (low), so the anchors occupied distinct source-native $\rhoload$ regimes. The separation persisted under fixed/recovered common loads: Harnett remained high, Popovic measured remained low, and Popovic model-fit remained low or intermediate. The Popovic measured uncertainty envelope remained low. Transfer ratios were treated only as compatibility proxies and not SPINE residuals. These results support a summary-level load-normalization hypothesis for part of the apparent high- versus low-isolation contrast, but they are not a per-spine reanalysis, not biological validation, not a prevalence estimate, and not a controversy resolution (Supplementary Figure S10).

\subsection{Residuals, amplitude, and somatic transfer require richer descriptors}

The residual analysis separated local-divider usefulness from broader prediction. For signed and absolute divider residuals, the dynamic ratio descriptor recorded as \texttt{dynamic\_SMI\_abs} in code/provenance tables was the strongest available scalar by rank association (absolute Spearman 0.853), while $\Rneck$ exceeded raw $\rhoload$ (about 0.765 versus 0.704). In rows with synaptic conductance scale, conductance plus log-component models sharply reduced residual prediction error. Residuals are therefore better treated as a dynamic/conductance/impedance problem than as evidence that the DC ratio alone is sufficient.

Head amplitude depended on a different descriptor family. Across non-exploratory rows, synaptic conductance scale had absolute Spearman association 0.982 with $\Ah$, whereas raw $\rhoload$ was only 0.143. Somatic transfer depended on downstream filtering: transfer gain and transfer impedance exceeded raw $\rhoload$ for $\Ghs$ in the descriptor summary. These results support a target-specific descriptor interpretation: local divider reasoning, head amplitude, residuals, and somatic transfer require different descriptor families.

Designed passive counterexamples sharpened this interpretation. Passive equal-$\rhoload$ cases with different morphology produced relative spread 0.28 in $\Ghd$ and 0.61 in $\Ah$, both exceeding the analysis-defined 20\% failure threshold. Equal $\rhoload$ is therefore not electrical equivalence, because the same ratio can hide different absolute resistances, capacitances, impedance spectra, synaptic drives, and downstream morphologies.

\subsection{Active and NMDA stress tests preserve scope limits}

Active-mechanism verification passed all 12 analysis-defined rows, including AMPA peak normalization, NMDA magnesium-block monotonicity, NMDA current-voltage relief, active-current signs, bounded gates, resting-state stability, timestep refinement, limiting-case solver comparison, strong-drive stability, and unit conversion checks. Active mechanisms were disabled in the reference baseline and enabled only in active-extension protocols.

In the designed active nonlinear suite, $\rhoload$ remained strongly associated with local $\Ghd$, but active mechanisms did not make the static ratio a general amplitude or somatic-transfer predictor (Figure~\ref{fig:active}). For active $\Ghs$, input impedance at 50 Hz exceeded raw $\rhoload$ (0.764 versus 0.339). For active $\Ah$, frozen-gate transfer gain exceeded raw $\rhoload$ (0.736 versus 0.236). AMPA+NMDA protocols with full restrained active conductances produced equal-$\rhoload$ relative spread 0.86 in $\Ah$ and 0.35 in $\Ghd$, exceeding the analysis-defined active stress-test thresholds.

Thus active mechanisms do not overturn the divider framing; they make its scope more important. Voltage-dependent current and active state add response variables that are not contained in a static morphology/load ratio.

\subsection{Deterministic uncertainty stabilizes sampled low/intermediate behavior but not high-ratio claims}

Progressive uncertainty analysis resolved the small-sample instability seen in the original 48-versus-96 comparison. By N=768, final $\rhoload$ median convergence passed: the 384-to-768 relative change was 0.012, below the analysis-defined 0.10 threshold. The maximum final median relative change across tracked outputs was 0.029. Exact top-predictor ranking remained less stable than descriptor-family interpretation.

The N=768 ensemble does not support high-$\rhoload$ uncertainty claims. It contained 757 low-$\rhoload$ rows, 11 intermediate-$\rhoload$ rows, and zero high-$\rhoload$ rows. A bounded high-$\rhoload$ extension added in the final revision sampled 41 true high-ratio parameter combinations and 205 deterministic radius-measurement rows; 16 crossed below $\rhoload=0.75$, giving a design flip fraction of 0.078, and the median peak residual remained negative at -0.3245. This extension samples the missing regime as a deterministic stress test, but it is not a replacement for a full uncertainty ensemble. High-isolation conclusions therefore rest on baseline reference, designed challenge rows, and explicitly labeled targeted checks.

Radius-only uncertainty produced 184 class flips out of 768 sampled parameter combinations, a design fraction of 24.0\%. Low-class combinations flipped in 22.9\% of cases, all 11 intermediate combinations flipped, and no high-$\rhoload$ combinations were present. Because $\Rneck$ scales as $1/r_n^2$, continuous ratio values with explicit radius uncertainty are safer than treating low, intermediate, and high labels as sharply separated biological states.

\subsection{Independent benchmarks support computational credibility within scope}

Computational credibility was assessed beyond internal scheme agreement. An independently written direct-matrix implementation of the same passive three-compartment baseline reproduced existing SPINE baseline traces to numerical round-off: the maximum all-trace absolute voltage difference was $1.25\times10^{-13}$ mV, the maximum head-amplitude difference was $1.42\times10^{-14}$ mV, and the maximum $\Ghd$ difference was $7.55\times10^{-15}$. DC analytic checks reproduced the two-node spine-omitted $\Rin$ value of 144.486692 M$\Omega$ to round-off and confirmed the divider limits.

Backward Euler versus Crank-Nicolson peak differences were small in the baseline reference cases: maximum peak differences were 0.00203 mV for $\Ah$, 0.000534 mV for $A_d$, 0.000189 mV for $A_s$, and 0.000500 for $\Ghd$. These are internal numerical self-consistency checks. Brian2 was installed as an optional project-local validation dependency and used for Brian2-hosted scoped checks of the three baseline cases, one distributed-neck passive case, and one AMPA/NMDA synapse-only case; all compared metrics passed predefined tolerances. Because the successful path used Brian2 for the validation environment and state hosting rather than native Brian2 integration of the full SPINE model, these checks are not all-row or active-channel cross-simulator validation. NEURON remains unavailable and is not claimed.

The final package includes manifest and checksum workflows and points to the verified public release at \url{https://github.com/blakepi/spine-divider-residuals}, release tag \texttt{v1.0.0-submission}, and archival DOI \url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20672333}.
