                                                    Abstract
           Spine-neck resistance is meaningful only relative to dendrite-soma load. We dene ρL =
        Rneck /Rin,d , with Rin,d measured after omitting the stimulated spine, and use Γdiv = 1/(1 + ρL )
        as the classical DC divider reference. Across 3,718 deterministic design-derived rows, peak local-
        transfer residuals were negative in 3,680/3,718 rows; pooled summaries are descriptive of the
        design table. Matched-time and integrated controls showed residual metric dependence. De-
        scriptor comparisons indicated that ρL is a compact local-divider coordinate, whereas residuals,
        head amplitude, and somatic transfer require component, impedance, conductance, active-state,
        and downstream-ltering descriptors. The N=768 uncertainty ensemble lacked high-ρL rows,
        so a bounded high-ρL stress test probed that regime without population inference. Direct-
        matrix/DC and scoped Brian2-hosted checks supported representative implementation behavior
        within scope. SPINE discourages inferring electrical isolation from spine-neck geometry alone.

Keywords:      dendritic spines; compartmental modeling; cable theory; voltage divider; electrical
isolation; impedance; reproducible modeling
    Dendritic spines place excitatory synapses in small postsynaptic compartments connected to
the parent dendrite by narrow necks. These necks can impede current ow, alter local voltage,
and separate spine-head depolarization from parent-dendrite or somatic impact (Rall, 1964; Segev
and Rall, 1988; Tsay and Yuste, 2004; Yuste, 2013; Araya et al., 2006; Harnett et al., 2012; Kwon
et al., 2017; Beaulieu-Laroche and Harnett, 2018; Cornejo et al., 2022). A neck resistance by itself,
however, is incomplete: the same series resistance has dierent consequences depending on the load
presented by the dendritic and somatic circuit.
    The rst-order electrical expectation is the classical voltage divider. If a spine-head voltage
drives a series neck resistance Rneck into a dendrite-soma load with input resistance Rin,d , the DC
small-signal local transfer is
                                               Rin,d             1
                                  Γdiv =                 =                  .                                (1)
                                           Rneck + Rin,d   1 + Rneck /Rin,d

For compact notation, we denote the load-normalized spine-neck ratio Rneck /Rin,d as ρL . The
abbreviation SMI is retained in some code paths, tables, and provenance les as author-dened
shorthand for the same ratio, not as a claim that the ratio is a eld-standard morphology index or
a new cable-theory law.

                                                        1
    This distinction matters because the divider expectation is not the same as a transient synap-
tic peak response. Conductance-based excitation changes driving force while the response evolves;
capacitance, local impedance, dendrite-to-soma ltering, active conductances, morphology, and mea-
surement uncertainty can all alter observed voltages (Magee, 2000; London and Hausser, 2005; Stuart
and Spruston, 2015; Popovic et al., 2015; Zecevic, 2023). A scalar ratio can be useful when the tar-
get is local low-frequency isolation, yet fail for head amplitude, somatic voltage, nonlinear response,
or categorical class assignment.
    The present study therefore reframes SPINE around the residual from the analytic divider:
                                        ∆div = Γh→d − Γdiv .                                       (2)
The ratio denes the expected local divider coordinate; the residual tests where the full simulated
response leaves that coordinate. This treats departures from the divider as domain-of-validity infor-
mation rather than failed conrmation. Large residuals, equal-ratio nonequivalence, and amplitude
failures identify the electrical variables that the compact ratio cannot contain.
     We ask ve bounded questions. First, do the workow-specied low-, intermediate-, and high-
isolation reference cases reproduce the intended passive responses? Second, how far do observed
peak local transfers depart from Γdiv across existing passive, active, uncertainty, and exploratory
rows? Third, when is the ratio more useful than its component resistances, and when do compo-
nent, impedance, dynamic, or conductance descriptors carry additional information? Fourth, how
should deterministic uncertainty and radius-error screens be reported without implying population
inference? Fifth, do independent matrix and analytic benchmarks support the computational cred-
ibility of the from-scratch passive implementation? The resulting thesis is deliberately scoped: the
load-normalized ratio recovers the classical local divider expectation, and SPINE maps the residual
domains where a single scalar is insucient.
     The practical implication is that experimental and modeling reports should not treat spine-neck
geometry alone as evidence for electrical isolation. They should report neck resistance together with
dendrite-soma load, use Γdiv as a transparent rst-order reference, prefer continuous ratio values
when radius uncertainty makes threshold classes fragile, and add timing, impedance, conductance,
and downstream-load descriptors before interpreting head amplitude or somatic impact.

1.2     Analytic divider and residual target

The analytic local reference is the DC small-signal voltage divider:
                                                 Rin,d          1
                                    Γdiv =                 =        .                            (5)
                                             Rneck + Rin,d   1 + ρL
This equation is treated as classical circuit logic, not as a novel result. The manuscript's main
diagnostic quantity is the residual between the simulated peak local transfer and this divider expec-
tation:
                                        ∆div = Γh→d − Γdiv .                                      (6)
Here Γh→d = Ad /Ah is measured from transient conductance-based simulations over the analysis-
dened metric window. In the primary residual table, Ah , Ad , and As are baseline-subtracted
maxima measured over the same 50 ms post-event window but not required to occur at the same
time. Thus Γh→d is a separate-peak transient summary, whereas Γdiv is a DC low-frequency reference.
Nonzero residuals are expected whenever peak timing, capacitance, changing synaptic driving force,
morphology, impedance, active state, or downstream coupling matters. Matched-time and area-
under-curve ratios are therefore reported only as targeted controls, not as replacements for the
manuscript's primary peak residual.

1.4   Challenge suites and descriptor comparisons

Passive morphology analyses generalize the reference circuit to compartment trees assembled from
nodes and axial edges. Frequency-domain analyses solve the linearized passive system to estimate
input impedance, transfer impedance, gain, phase, and dynamic ratio variants. Active and NMDA
mechanisms are disabled in the baseline reference conguration and enabled only in active-extension
protocols. These active conductances are generic stress tests using literature-traceable current forms
(Hodgkin and Huxley, 1952; Mayer et al., 1984; Jahr and Stevens, 1990; Magee, 1998, 1999; Con-
nor and Stevens, 1971; Huguenard and McCormick, 1992), not calibrated cell-type-specic channel
densities.
    Descriptor comparisons were performed on existing derived rows. We compared raw ρL , the
analytic divider transform Γdiv , component resistances Rneck and Rin,d , log-component models,
impedance and dynamic descriptors, transfer gain, and synaptic conductance scale where avail-
able. These comparisons were interpreted by target and descriptor family. Exact winner labels were
avoided when near-tied or correlated predictors made the ranking unstable.
    Designed counterexamples tested logical suciency. Equal-ρL cases ask whether equal ratio
implies equivalent voltage responses; iso-neck-resistance cases ask whether equal Rneck can change
under dierent loads; iso-transfer cases ask whether similar transfer can arise from dierent ratios.
These experiments were designed to expose limitations as well as successes.

1.5   Deterministic uncertainty and reporting language

Uncertainty analyses used deterministic Latin-hypercube ensembles and progressive sample sizes
of N=96, 192, 384, and 768. The global uncertainty seed was 202605; progressive ensembles used
202605 + 5100 + N . Radius-only uncertainty was analyzed separately because Rneck ∝ 1/rn2 , using
25 deterministic osets spanning ±2 SD with SD = 0.018 µm and clipping measured radius to
0.060.22 µm. Heuristic ratio classes were low < 0.25, intermediate 0.25 to < 0.75, and high ≥ 0.75.
    All uncertainty fractions are reported as deterministic design fractions over sampled parameter
combinations. They are not biological rates and are not treated as inferential population intervals.
The N=768 ensemble contained 757 low-ρL rows, 11 intermediate-ρL rows, and zero high-ρL rows,
so high-ratio uncertainty claims are explicitly scoped to reference, designed challenge rows, and the
labeled targeted high-ρL extension rather than to the main uncertainty ensemble.

1.7   Computational credibility checks

Numerical credibility was assessed at three levels. First, internal checks covered units, current
signs, synaptic normalization, limiting cases, timestep renement, sparse/dense passive assembly,
impedance verication, and Backward Euler versus Crank-Nicolson peak dierences. Second, an
independent direct-matrix benchmark separately assembled the baseline passive three-compartment
equations and compared against existing SPINE trace outputs without calling the production pas-
sive simulation function as its benchmark engine. Third, DC analytic checks tested the two-node
dendrite-soma input resistance, divider formula, limiting cases, and attached-versus-omitted one-
port behavior.
    NEURON and Brian2 importability were checked in the default project runtime. NEURON
was unavailable and is not claimed. Brian2 was unavailable in that runtime but was installed in
a project-local optional validation environment, where it was used with explicit units, a Brian2
clock/state container, and an independent SI backward-Euler update. The Brian2-hosted scoped
checks covered the three passive baseline cases, one cylindrical distributed-neck passive case, and
one AMPA/NMDA synapse-only case. Native Brian2 state updaters were attempted but not re-
tained because explicit/RK updates were unstable for the sti compact head-neck system and the
coupled symbolic linear updater was not practical in this local environment. The executed checks
therefore support representative computational credibility within scope; they do not validate all
rows, restrained active channels, or biological calibration.
    Environment, optional Brian2 versions, solver method, timestep, analysis window, deterministic
seeds, release tag, and DOI are summarized in Supplementary Table S7.

2     Results
2.1    Reference cases reproduce the intended passive ordering

The baseline reference conguration generated the intended progression from near-isopotential head-
dendrite responses to strong local isolation (Figures 1 and 2). The simulations used the baseline
parameters in Table 1, ∆t = 0.01 ms, a single head synapse at 20 ms, and the analysis-dened
50 ms metric window. All nine low-, intermediate-, and high-isolation target values passed the
workow-specied tolerance rule.
    The low-ρL case produced ρL = 0.00881, Γh→d = 0.984, Γh→s = 0.357, and Ah = 1.82 mV. The
intermediate case produced ρL = 0.115, Γh→d = 0.751, Γh→s = 0.273, and Ah = 2.36 mV. The
high-ρL case produced ρL = 1.32, Γh→d = 0.102, Γh→s = 0.0376, and Ah = 15.1 mV. These target
cases support implementation consistency for the reference equations, units, synaptic normalization,
solver, and metric denitions in the compact passive architecture; they do not calibrate the model
to a specic biological neuron.
    The same traces show why the divider residual is needed. The baseline divider predictions were
0.991, 0.897, and 0.431 for the low, intermediate, and high cases. The corresponding residuals
Γh→d − Γdiv were -0.00729, -0.1458, and -0.3286. The low case was close to the DC divider, whereas
the intermediate and high transient peak ratios fell well below it.

      2.3                            Load normalization is useful but compact

   The xed-load geometry sweep behaved as a constructional sanity check. Longer and narrower necks
   increased Rneck , increased head depolarization, and decreased downstream transfer. Because Rin,d
   was xed, ρL and Rneck had identical rank ordering. This result should not be read as evidence that
   the ratio adds information under xed-load conditions.
       The matched-neck experiment held Rneck = 100 MΩ while varying dendritic leak from 0.1 to
   30 nS (Figure 4). Under this manipulation, neck resistance alone could not rank the cases because
   there was only one neck-resistance value. Changing the dendritic load changed Rin,d , and therefore
   changed ρL and downstream transfer. The load term is therefore mechanistically meaningful, but
   only within the target it was designed to represent.
       Descriptor comparisons made this compactness explicit. For non-exploratory local transfer, the
   load-normalized ratio and Γdiv had the same absolute Spearman association because the divider is
   a monotone transform of the ratio, but the analytic divider was the better direct scalar prediction
   (cross-validated RMSE 0.052 versus 0.091 for raw ρL ). The log component pair improved on raw ρL
   but did not outperform the analytic divider for rst-order local transfer. Thus the ratio is useful as
   the coordinate dening the divider, not as a universally superior scalar.

2.4   Residuals, amplitude, and somatic transfer require richer descriptors

The residual analysis separated local-divider usefulness from broader prediction. For signed
and absolute divider residuals, the dynamic ratio descriptor recorded as dynamic_SMI_abs in
code/provenance tables was the strongest available scalar by rank association (absolute Spearman
0.853), while Rneck exceeded raw ρL (about 0.765 versus 0.704). In rows with synaptic conductance
scale, conductance plus log-component models sharply reduced residual prediction error. Residuals
are therefore better treated as a dynamic/conductance/impedance problem than as evidence that
the DC ratio alone is sucient.
    Head amplitude depended on a dierent descriptor family. Across non-exploratory rows, synaptic
conductance scale had absolute Spearman association 0.982 with Ah , whereas raw ρL was only 0.143.
Somatic transfer depended on downstream ltering: transfer gain and transfer impedance exceeded
raw ρL for Γh→s in the descriptor summary. These results support a target-specic descriptor
interpretation: local divider reasoning, head amplitude, residuals, and somatic transfer require
dierent descriptor families.
    Designed passive counterexamples sharpened this interpretation. Passive equal-ρL cases with
dierent morphology produced relative spread 0.28 in Γh→d and 0.61 in Ah , both exceeding the
analysis-dened 20% failure threshold. Equal ρL is therefore not electrical equivalence, because the
same ratio can hide dierent absolute resistances, capacitances, impedance spectra, synaptic drives,
and downstream morphologies.

2.5   Active and NMDA stress tests preserve scope limits

Active-mechanism verication passed all 12 analysis-dened rows, including AMPA peak normal-
ization, NMDA magnesium-block monotonicity, NMDA current-voltage relief, active-current signs,
bounded gates, resting-state stability, timestep renement, limiting-case solver comparison, strong-
drive stability, and unit conversion checks. Active mechanisms were disabled in the reference baseline
and enabled only in active-extension protocols.
    In the designed active nonlinear suite, ρL remained strongly associated with local Γh→d , but
active mechanisms did not make the static ratio a general amplitude or somatic-transfer predictor
(Figure 6). For active Γh→s , input impedance at 50 Hz exceeded raw ρL (0.764 versus 0.339). For
active Ah , frozen-gate transfer gain exceeded raw ρL (0.736 versus 0.236). AMPA+NMDA protocols
with full restrained active conductances produced equal-ρL relative spread 0.86 in Ah and 0.35 in
Γh→d , exceeding the analysis-dened active stress-test thresholds.
    Thus active mechanisms do not overturn the divider framing; they make its scope more impor-
tant. Voltage-dependent current and active state add response variables that are not contained in
a static morphology/load ratio.

2.6   Deterministic uncertainty stabilizes sampled low/intermediate behavior but
      not high-ratio claims

Progressive uncertainty analysis resolved the small-sample instability seen in the original 48-versus-
96 comparison. By N=768, nal ρL median convergence passed: the 384-to-768 relative change was
0.012, below the analysis-dened 0.10 threshold. The maximum nal median relative change across
tracked outputs was 0.029. Exact top-predictor ranking remained less stable than descriptor-family
interpretation.
    The N=768 ensemble does not support high-ρL uncertainty claims. It contained 757 low-ρL
rows, 11 intermediate-ρL rows, and zero high-ρL rows. A bounded high-ρL extension added in
the nal revision sampled 41 true high-ratio parameter combinations and 205 deterministic radius-
measurement rows; 16 crossed below ρL = 0.75, giving a design ip fraction of 0.078, and the
median peak residual remained negative at -0.3245. This extension samples the missing regime
as a deterministic stress test, but it is not a replacement for a full uncertainty ensemble. High-
isolation conclusions therefore rest on baseline reference, designed challenge rows, and explicitly
labeled targeted checks.
    Radius-only uncertainty produced 184 class ips out of 768 sampled parameter combinations,
a design fraction of 24.0%. Low-class combinations ipped in 22.9% of cases, all 11 intermediate
combinations ipped, and no high-ρL combinations were present. Because Rneck scales as 1/rn2 ,
continuous ratio values with explicit radius uncertainty are safer than treating low, intermediate,
and high labels as sharply separated biological states.

2.7   Independent benchmarks support computational credibility within scope

Computational credibility was assessed beyond internal scheme agreement. An independently writ-
ten direct-matrix implementation of the same passive three-compartment baseline reproduced exist-
ing SPINE baseline traces to numerical round-o: the maximum all-trace absolute voltage dierence

3    Discussion
SPINE now supports a residual-domain interpretation of the load-normalized spine-neck ratio. The
ratio ρL = Rneck /Rin,d encodes the classical local divider expectation Γdiv = 1/(1+ρL ); SMI remains
only as legacy shorthand for this quantity in code-facing outputs. The scientic work begins after
that analytic reference is stated: transient conductance-based simulations usually produced peak
local transfer below the DC divider, and the residuals identify where a single scalar loses information.
    This reframing is important because it separates a true but limited electrical expectation from
broader prediction. The low-ρL reference case was close to the divider limit, while intermediate and
high reference cases were substantially below the divider in peak transfer. Across the full derived
table, residuals were usually negative and sometimes large for the separate-peak metric. That
pattern is not a failure to reproduce the divider; it is evidence that the divider is the low-frequency
baseline and that peak synaptic responses live in a richer dynamic system.
    The practical use pattern is therefore two-step. Use the load-normalized ratio to report rst-pass
local head-to-dendrite divider context. Then report residuals, component resistances, impedance,
transfer gain, synaptic conductance scale, active state, morphology, and uncertainty whenever the
target is peak transfer, head amplitude, somatic voltage, nonlinear response, class membership,
or electrical equivalence. For experimental interpretation, that means reporting neck resistance
together with dendritic/somatic load rather than geometry alone, and treating Γdiv as a transparent
reference rather than as evidence that an observed transient response has been explained.
    The ratio succeeds locally because it compares a series neck resistance with the load driven by
that resistance. This is the classical cable-theoretic intuition for spine-neck isolation (Rall, 1964;
Segev and Rall, 1988; Yuste, 2013; Magee, 2000). It also explains why the matched-neck control is
meaningful: when Rneck is xed, dierent dendritic loads produce dierent divider expectations.


                                                   15
     The same logic explains why the ratio fails outside its target. Head amplitude depends on
absolute synaptic conductance, driving-force reduction, local capacitance, and input impedance.
Somatic transfer adds dendrite-to-soma ltering, a distinction related to location-dependent den-
dritic propagation and somatic EPSP normalization (Magee and Cook, 2000). Active state, NMDA
voltage dependence, clustered input, and timing can change voltage trajectories without changing
the morphology-derived ratio. Equal ρL cannot imply electrical equivalence because many circuits
share a ratio while diering in absolute RC constants, impedance phase, conductance drive, and
morphology.
     This does not make the ratio useless. It makes it conditional. A scalar descriptor can be valuable
when its target is explicit and its residuals are measured. It becomes misleading only when the local
divider coordinate is stretched into an amplitude, somatic, nonlinear, or categorical claim.
     The radius audit highlights a measurement boundary. Because Rneck ∝ 1/rn2 , modest radius error
can produce large resistance and ratio error, and spine-neck geometry is itself plastic and dicult
to measure precisely (Tønnesen et al., 2014; Tønnesen and Nägerl, 2016). The N=768 radius screen
produced a 24.0% design-fraction class ip, with all 11 intermediate-ρL combinations crossing a
class boundary. This supports continuous reporting with explicit uncertainty more strongly than
thresholded low/intermediate/high labels.
     The same N=768 ensemble contained no high-ρL rows. This absence should be treated as a
design limitation. The targeted high-ρL extension added here is useful as a bounded stress test, but
it is not a substitute for a full uncertainty ensemble spanning that regime. High-isolation behavior
is therefore supported by reference cases, designed challenge rows, and targeted checks, whereas
uncertainty stability is supported only for sampled low/intermediate combinations.
     The from-scratch passive implementation is computationally credible within the scope tested. In-
ternal unit, current-balance, limiting-case, timestep, impedance, and BE-versus-CN checks support
numerical self-consistency. More importantly for external credibility, an independent direct-matrix
implementation reproduced baseline passive traces and metrics to round-o, and DC analytic bench-
marks checked the dendrite-soma load and divider limits.
     Brian2 added a useful optional implementation-check anchor, but the scope remains narrow.
Brian2 was installed in an isolated validation environment and the Brian2-hosted scoped checks re-
produced representative passive baselines, one distributed-neck passive case, and one AMPA/NMDA
synapse-only case within predened tolerances. However, native Brian2 state updaters were not the
nal successful integration path for the sti compact circuit, and restrained active channels were
not reproduced in Brian2. The strongest supported statement is therefore narrower than global
simulator validation: the passive reference equations are consistent with independent matrix/DC
benchmarks and representative Brian2-hosted checks. Extending native cross-simulator checks to
broader morphologies, active protocols, and uncertainty rows remains useful future work.
     The results t naturally with mixed experimental evidence about spine electrical isolation.
Some optical and uncaging studies support substantial compartmentalization in particular contexts,
whereas other work emphasizes modest isolation, indicator limits, and context-dependent interpre-
tation (Araya et al., 2006; Kwon et al., 2017; Cornejo et al., 2022; Popovic et al., 2015; Zecevic,
2023). A manuscript that reports only a single ratio would atten these dierences. A manuscript
that reports the ratio, divider expectation, residuals, and target-specic descriptors can connect
morphology to physiology without overclaiming.
     The summary-level external decision gate reinforces the reporting lesson without broadening
the validation claim. The machine-recoverable Harnett and Popovic anchors suggest that some
apparent high- versus low-isolation contrast can be reframed in load-normalized coordinates, but the
evidence remains summary-level only: Harnett is a model/summary anchor rather than recovered
paired measured per-spine data, Popovic measured and model-t rows are kept separate, Kwon

                                                  16
and Cornejo remain context/exclusion rows, and transfer proxies are not SPINE residuals. The
overlay motivates reporting dendritic load together with neck resistance, but it is not a per-spine
reanalysis, not biological validation, not a prevalence estimate, and not a controversy resolution.
A true empirical test would require paired same-spine Rneck , local dendritic load, and transfer
denitions from the same preparation.
    The current framework remains intentionally limited. The baseline soma is a lumped down-
stream load, passive morphologies are procedural rather than cell-specic reconstructions, active
conductances are generic stress tests rather than calibrated densities, and uncertainty ensembles
are deterministic sensitivity designs rather than posterior biological distributions. SPINE focuses
on electrical behavior and does not model biochemical calcium reaction-diusion, receptor track-
ing, signaling cascades, or structural plasticity.
    The most valuable next studies would combine voltage imaging, morphology measurements,
and electrophysiology in preparations where neck-radius uncertainty can be propagated explicitly.
Modeling extensions should prioritize calibrated cell-type active conductances, morphology-diverse
posterior inference, analysis-dened high-ρL uncertainty coverage, and fuller native cross-simulator
checks. Accordingly, the strongest supported claim is that the divider is expected and the residuals
are informative.
    The load-normalized spine-neck ratio is therefore a compact coordinate for the classical local
voltage divider, not a universal electrical phenotype. Its most defensible use is to state the expected
low-frequency head-to-dendrite transfer and then measure the residual from that expectation. In
SPINE, those residuals show when transient conductance dynamics, impedance, synaptic drive,
active state, morphology, downstream ltering, and measurement uncertainty make the scalar de-
scriptor incomplete.


4    Conclusion
The load-normalized spine-neck ratio is a compact coordinate for the classical local voltage divider.
Its most defensible use is to state the expected low-frequency head-to-dendrite transfer, Γdiv =
1/(1 + ρL ), and then measure the residual from that expectation. In SPINE, those residuals are
the main scientic object: they show when transient conductance dynamics, impedance, synaptic
drive, active state, morphology, downstream ltering, and measurement uncertainty make the scalar
descriptor incomplete. The practical rule is therefore simple: use the ratio for rst-pass local divider
reasoning, and use residuals plus richer electrical descriptors for peak transfer, head amplitude,
somatic impact, nonlinear response, thresholded classes, and equivalence claims.
